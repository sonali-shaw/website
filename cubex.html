Cubex
Cubex started as a backend representation of a Rubik's cube and its moves — pure state and move logic, no visuals. A Three.js web version came next (playable above, right in your browser), followed by a full native iOS app: a 3D cube rendered with SceneKit that you can turn by dragging any sticker, orbit and pinch-to-zoom around, plus a complete solver that walks through the classic layer-by-layer method one human-readable step at a time, with a "play all" mode, an undo/back step, and a built-in guide explaining the cube's notation.

Technical Deep Dive
How Cubex Works
Cube Model & Moves

The cube is represented as six faces, each a 3×3 grid of color letters — no physical geometry, just state. Every notation move (F+, F-, T2, ...) reduces to a small set of primitive operations: rotating a single face's grid, and re-mapping the four adjacent side strips that border it. Whole-cube reorientations reuse the same primitives. This model was originally written in Python, then ported line-for-line to Swift for the app and to JavaScript for the browser version — the same logic runs, unchanged in behavior, in three different languages.

3D Rendering & Interaction

The visible cube is 26 individually rendered "cubie" nodes in SceneKit, each a rounded box with six independently colored materials. Turning a face works by temporarily re-parenting the affected cubies under a pivot node, animating that pivot's rotation, then snapping every cubie's grid coordinates back to exact integers once the animation finishes — so floating-point drift never accumulates across repeated turns.

  • Drag-to-turn is resolved geometrically: a touch's on-screen movement is projected against the two candidate rotation axes tangent to the sticker you grabbed, and a cross-product sign check picks the correct turn direction — verified with dedicated geometry unit tests, not just eyeballed
  • The camera is a hand-built spherical orbit (radius/azimuth/elevation) rather than SceneKit's stock controls, specifically so the cube is structurally guaranteed to stay centered no matter how far you orbit or zoom
The Solver

Implements the classic five-step layer-by-layer method from James Nourse's The Simple Solution to Rubik's Cube. The first three steps translate directly into code. The last two rely on matching the cube's pattern against one of a handful of book diagrams to pick a follow-up sequence — instead of transcribing those diagrams, the solver runs a small breadth-first search over candidate whole-cube rotations on deep copies of the cube, stopping as soon as one reaches the solved (or partially solved) target pattern. Every move it makes is logged as a human-readable instruction, which is what powers the step-by-step solve UI: Next Step and Back replay or invert one recorded instruction at a time, and a manual turn mid-solve is detected and auto-corrected rather than silently breaking the sequence.
